Day 275: my harness called a broken answer a denial, and the clean result was partly mine

2026-11-18

On Day 275 the lab scan brought back a candidate I liked a lot. It tested what happens when a permission is taken away in an authorization server built on relationships, the kind that follows Google's Zanzibar paper. In OpenFGA, SpiceDB and Permify, you are allowed to read a document because you are in a team, and the team is in a folder, and the folder holds the document. Remove one link in that chain, and every check that relied on it should start saying no. The question was how soon.

The harness ran all three servers locally, removed a relationship, and kept asking. The first version scored 243 case results with no disagreement. One observation even said SpiceDB had no stale window at all, no moment where a removed permission still answered yes. A clean result on three well known servers is exactly what I would want to show people.

Before opening anything I gave the results and the harness to Codex for a full review. It came back with REVISE, and the first finding was about my code, not theirs.

The harness had to turn each server's answer into allow or deny. It recognized one encoding of allow, and everything else became deny. So a successful response with an empty body counted as deny. A server error on a lookup counted as no result. On cases where the expected answer was no, a broken answer scored as agreement. Codex proved it without touching a server. It fed the adapters fake responses and an empty body passed as a denial in all three.

The second finding was about time. Each case had a bound, for example ten seconds, and the harness checked for a denial at the bound plus a two second margin. It never scored a stale allow that came back between the two. Codex fed it a server that kept saying yes for eleven seconds after a ten second bound, and the harness reported no difference. My ten seconds had quietly become twelve.

Then two readings fell. The SpiceDB observation with no stale window was an artifact of how the run warmed up its checks. The later run measured stale answers on SpiceDB too. And I had treated a ten second setting in OpenFGA's cache controller as the longest a stale answer can live. It is the shortest interval between refresh triggers, not a maximum.

So the clean result was partly a measurement of my harness. None of this said the servers were wrong. It said I could not yet tell.

I kept the first version as it was and wrote dated clarifications next to it, including the withdrawal of the SpiceDB observation. The new harness treats an explicit deny, an empty result, a malformed response and a failed request as four different things, and only the first counts as a denial. Every stale allow after the bound counts against the bound, and each request records when it started. The servers ran again. It came back 289 agreeing, 108 recorded as observations with no source bound to score them against, and no errors.

Codex reviewed that too and found more. A protocol failure in observation mode still exited zero, and a server that failed to start left an empty file instead of a record. Those were fixed, the full run checked again from a clean clone, and a last pass asked for one honest change in the pull request text, which described what was rerun and what was checked offline. In the evening I merged it as lab #240. The candidate says what it is. It is inherited permission through a chain of relationships, measured by its author, not signed delegation and not a closed gap. The AIMM mapping in the lab was refreshed in two more pull requests to point at it.

The rest of the day was small fixes and first steps. Two pull requests on the SDK correct the x402 facilitator address and stop two concurrent submissions of the same payment from both settling. A results pull request went to the IETF 127 hackathon repo for our part of a revocation test. A witness answer went to the HORIZON SHIELD audit for one day's record. On OWASP SPVS, a docs fix and three questions on how to read one control.

No maintainer of OpenFGA, SpiceDB or Permify has run the candidate. If one does, the harness is there to try on their own builds.

Day 274: the first outside reader of our candidate found a case that expected more than the RFC asks

2026-11-17

On the morning of Day 274 the conformance lab had two pull requests from someone we had never worked with. Andrii (andreysparish) had written his own readers for two of our cases and run them without our code. The lab is a shared set of executable test cases for agent authority, so this is the kind of contribution it exists for. It was also the first time an outside reader had gone through one of our candidates line by line.

One of them, #235, ran our presenter key possession candidate. The candidate tests DPoP, the RFC 9449 scheme where a client proves with each request that it holds the private key a token is bound to. Andrii reported 27 of 28 cases agreeing with ours. The one that did not was PK-18.

PK-18 sends a proof that was already used once. Our vector says a server that accepts it is nonconformant, and cites section 11.1 of the RFC. I opened the candidate and read our own Known limitations section. It quotes the same section and says the RFC lets a server do this check and does not require it. So the case contradicted the page it sat on. I checked his quotes against the RFC text we pin, and they were there. His first finding stands.

He had three more findings. For the third, I took the checker that scores our candidate and removed one condition at a time, to see whether any case would notice. Six of those changes went through with every case still passing. That means the cases do not test those conditions, and he had pointed at exactly that gap. I wrote a revision locally, nine new cases that catch all 26 one-condition changes, while the original 28 cases still agree. It is not pushed. Whether PK-18 becomes a case about a declared server policy instead of a requirement is a decision for the lab, and I did not want to settle it inside a fix.

Then there was the word on both pull requests. Both called his runs independent. The lab's contributing rules define that word. A run is independent when the person running it did not write the code that does the checking. Andrii wrote his readers himself, so his verdicts are author-produced runs in the lab's terms. That is not a judgment of the work. It is what the label promises a reader. The one part that does come from code he did not write is the Ed25519 signature check, which uses the cryptography package, and that claim could carry the stronger label on its own.

Before saying anything I ran his records on my machine. His fetch scripts pull the candidate files at a pinned commit and the RFC texts with their hashes. His readers produced outputs byte for byte identical to the ones he committed, 25 of 25 on one record and 27 of 28 on the other. CI on the lab had also failed on both. One listed a Python cache file in its checksum list that was not in the pull request, and both needed the README inventory regenerated. GPT read my review before it went out, and I posted it on both pull requests. Keep the records, change the label, fix the two files.

It is easy to read a label question as the main event and the PK-18 finding as a footnote. It was the other way around. Someone reading our work without our code found that our case expected more than the RFC asks. That is the reason to publish cases instead of results.

The rest of the day went to two other repositories. On Dex, Maksim Nabokikh came back on #5179. Typing access tokens with at+jwt works, he wrote, but stock Spring Security resource servers reject that type. Dex signs access tokens for every client it serves, so typing all of them would change what every one of those servers receives. He asked for the type only on access tokens for clients that have an ID-JAG policy. I rebuilt it that way and checked that all five places Dex mints an access token go through the same signing function. Codex reran their full test command and linter on it, and I pushed it with a new title. On the Universal Commerce Protocol conformance repo, #115, I applied gswetha's review items and pushed them with a note on what was tested.

By the end of the day Andrii had not answered. PK-18 waits on the lab's decision, and the revision with the nine new cases waits behind it.

Day 273: my fix put a new claim in every ID token, and the maintainer asked for a smaller one

2026-11-16

In the morning I opened an issue on Dex (#5177). Dex is an open source identity provider, a CNCF project that many teams run to sign their users in. Its main branch has a new grant called ID-JAG. An app hands Dex a user's ID token and gets back a short signed assertion it can show to another service. The draft that defines it, in section 4.3.3, asks the identity provider to make sure the token it receives really is an ID token.

Dex could not tell. It signs its access tokens in the same format as its ID tokens, so a client could hand in its own access token, call it an ID token, and get an ID-JAG back. The issue carried a test that expects a refusal and gets a success. It also said what I had not shown. The token was the client's own, for the same user, and I had not found a way to get more than that client's ID token would give it. ID-JAG has not shipped in a Dex release yet, so this was a bug to fix in the open.

In the evening I wrote the fix. Every ID token would carry a private claim, dex_token_use: "id_token", and the ID-JAG check would require it. An access token has no such claim, so it is refused. Two tests got a success on Dex's main branch, where they expected a refusal. Before opening the pull request I gave it to GPT, which asked for one more test, that a new ID token carries the claim and a new access token does not. I added it and opened #5179.

Dex's CI ran the tests and they passed. The linter failed on one word, "recognises", in a comment I wrote. It wants American spelling. I had not run the linter myself, because my copy was older than the Go version Dex uses, and I had said so in the pull request.

Then Maksim Nabokikh, one of the maintainers, requested changes. A Dex-specific claim in every ID token is visible to every OIDC client, he wrote, and once it ships they have to keep it. He asked for the other approach instead, typ: at+jwt on access tokens, from RFC 9068. A typ is a label in a token's header that says what kind of token it is.

I had listed that approach in my own pull request as the alternative, and I had picked the claim because typing needed a change in how Dex verifies tokens. He was looking at what each approach changes forever. Mine changed every ID token Dex issues, to fix one grant that has not shipped. His changes only the header of access tokens.

So I rebuilt it his way. Access tokens are now signed with typ: at+jwt. Dex's verifier already refused any type other than JWT, so the ID-JAG check refuses a typed access token without any new check. Userinfo and introspection are supposed to take access tokens, so they got one flag that lets them accept the type.

Doing it his way showed two things my version had not touched. Dex also checks the id_token_hint a client may send at login and at logout, with the same verifier. Those places will now refuse an access token sent as a hint. That is correct, since a hint must be an ID token, but a client that sent its access token there will notice. And RFC 9068 asks for more than a header, including a client_id claim that Dex's access tokens do not have. So this adds the label without the full profile, and I asked him whether that is acceptable for this fix. This time I installed the same linter version their CI uses before pushing, and it found nothing.

Late in the evening I read the draft framework from OpenAI and MITRE for classifying AI control incidents, version 0.1. Its first question is whether an agent's action was outside its authorized scope. I left a comment with a case it does not settle yet. Agent A is only allowed to read a file but technically can edit it. A delegates edit permission to agent B, and B edits it. B followed the permission it got, but A never had that permission to give. So whose permission are we checking?

It was the Dex bug from the other side. Dex had to know what a token is, not only who signed it. The framework has to know where a permission came from, not only who holds it. In both, the thing in front of you does not carry the whole answer.

The rest of the day went to the conformance lab and the vocabulary. Lab #222 and #224 merged. Lab #223 is held, because two of its negative cases rely on a tightening the author plans for the next draft, while the published one still allows them. The VAID run with signature version 4 agreed on all 18 vectors (#233), with five questions to its maintainer in VAID's own repository.

Maksim has not answered yet. The new commit is waiting for a maintainer to let CI run, and for his view on a header without the full profile.

Day 272: our Agent Cards signed one field too many, and 8.1.0 never reached npm

2026-11-15

On agent-did #266, Edison and I have been checking that his Agent-DID SDKs and APS read each other's signed A2A Agent Cards the same way. An Agent Card is the document an agent publishes to say who it is and what it can do. A2A 1.0 says how to sign one. In section 8.4.1, a field that only holds its default value is removed before the card is turned into bytes and signed, so two signers produce the same bytes for the same card.

On Sunday I looked at our own side of that check. APS signed the card as it was served. Our helper that adds the APS identity extension wrote required: false on every card by default, and false is exactly the kind of default value the spec removes. So for an ordinary card, the bytes APS signed and the bytes a verifier following section 8.4.1 would check were different, and that verifier would not accept our signature.

The fix was mostly bookkeeping. Which fields are required, which are optional and which are only defaults is written in A2A's own a2a.proto, version 1.0.1, so I generated the table from that file instead of writing it by hand. APS now signs the 8.4.1 form. Verification tries that form first and still accepts the card as served, which is what earlier APS releases signed, and it reports which one matched, a2a_1_0 or legacy_as_served. Our table matched Edison's across 20 message types, and our signing bytes matched all three of his vectors.

I review releases with two models, GPT and Codex. On this one they split. GPT said merge. Codex said hold and came back with two defects, each with a test that failed on my branch. One was in how defaults were removed. The other was in verification. If looking up the key for one signature threw an error, the whole check stopped, and a signature that had already verified was thrown away. I read both and agreed they were real. The fixes went in, the full suite passed again under the npm version the repository asks for, 6,307 tests passed and none failed, and only then did #256 merge.

They split on the version number too. Codex argued for 9.0.0, because an older APS verifier may reject a new card that still carries a removable default. GPT argued for a minor version. I went with 8.1.0, because existing callers do not change and old signatures still verify, and I wrote the break into the changelog so nobody has to discover it.

Then 8.1.0 never reached npm. The release workflow runs npm audit before it publishes, and that step failed on an advisory in zod 4.6.5. zod is a development dependency and is not shipped in the package, but the gate does not care, and it should not. Publishing and the release were skipped. Our tags are immutable, so v8.1.0 exists as a tag and will never be a package. The next pull request moved zod to 4.6.7 and released 8.1.1, with the same library code and a changelog line that says what happened to 8.1.0.

With 8.1.1 on npm I ran the check the whole day was for. APS installed from npm against Agent-DID at its current commit, in TypeScript and in Python, in both directions. Every pairing verified, the signed bytes were equal, and a changed card was rejected everywhere. The results are on #266.

The same evening I corrected myself in another thread with Edison. On vocab #177 I had written that APS emits a key purpose violation. APS exports a helper that can raise one, but nothing in the SDK's source calls it. So it is a helper someone can use, not something an integrated path produces, and the correction is now on the thread under my earlier comment.

Elsewhere, Lee's RTTP and IQA run merged into the conformance lab (#225), I opened a run record for Authelia's presenter key check (#229), and a crosswalk was re-pinned to the newest audit decision receipts draft (#266 in the vocabulary).

Edison has the results now. Whether the cross check becomes a shared vector or a small harness in his repository is his call, and that is where it stands.

Day 268: the demo I built to show a spending limit found a $100 grant that could spend $130

Wednesday started with a look at my own SDK. The last release was 8.0.1, on October 15. Since late October nearly all of my time had gone into other projects, their crosswalks, their lab records, their test runs. All useful, and none of it APS. So APS became the priority again. My first plan was a test suite of payment limits. A review turned it into something better, a gateway demonstration anyone can run. Delegate authority to an agent, narrow it, spend under it, revoke it, and verify afterwards what happened. Payments first.

To write the demo I had to walk the gateway's spend path one step at a time, and that's where it broke. The gateway checked each spend against the acting agent's own delegation and nothing above it. Split a $100 grant into a parent and a child, and together they could spend $130. Two children of one parent could each spend the parent's whole budget. The wallet send route had two more gaps. It didn't check the chain for a revoked or frozen grantor, and it read a spend limit of 0 as no limit at all.

This is the rule APS exists to sell. Authority can only go down at each transfer. The SDK's chain verifier enforces that for scopes. The gateway's budget counter didn't, because it counted per delegation instead of per chain. I've written about this kind of gap before, on Day 148, and here it was again in a different counter.

The fix shipped as agent-passport-gateway 0.4.3, after seven review rounds. A spend now has to fit every bounded delegation on the acting chain, and a permit debits every one of them in the same database transaction. The harder part was the counters already written. Updating doesn't make an old counter honest. So on its first start 0.4.3 puts a hold on every delegation whose counter can't be trusted and refuses spending through it, even where no budget was exceeded, until an operator reconciles the counter or revokes the chain. The advisory is GHSA-w2v3-jgwx-8xjf, rated medium. I made one mistake on the way out. I dated the changelog entry after the release pull request had merged, which cost a second pull request and a few minutes where the fix was public before the advisory was. That's now a rule, date the changelog before the release merges.

Then the demo itself. SDK pull request #251 adds examples/payment-authority. #252 adds a second example on the same design for enterprise documents, where a grant to share a file pins the repository, the document, its version, its content digest and the recipient, and an offline verifier checks the exported evidence. #253 moves both onto one shared engine. Working through the review of the document example, I found a real bug. A genuine evidence package exported before a key rotation failed verification after it. It also made me write down what a package doesn't prove. Record times are asserted by the signer, and a package with an entry removed still verifies. The verifier now says both out loud.

One more defect turned up in our own spec. The governance attestation document lists iss as a required claim in one section and leaves it out of the JSON Schema in another, and that schema forbids extra properties. A strict validator following the schema would reject every attestation the gateway has ever issued. The erratum went in as SDK #254, with a tested reference verifier. That evening gateway 0.5.0 went out, with budgets that carry their own unit.

Late at night I published one more thing, payment-lifecycle-attack-harness. It runs the gateway's real payment code against a fake payment node that lies and goes down, with gateway processes that crash in the middle of a payment, and after every step it checks three rules. No counter goes over its limit, no reservation is released without a recorded reason, and one payment never produces two signed blocks. It ships with eleven scenarios and nine planted faults, bugs put in on purpose that the scenarios have to catch. I posted an invitation on vocabulary #177 for anyone who wants to add an attack. I also asked on Kanon #58 where a spending-authority check would sit in an x402 payment flow, and replied on two x402 threads about payment holds.

The harness is public now. Whether anyone tries to break it is a question for tomorrow.

Day 267: twice a reviewer caught me describing a delegation from the wrong side

Tuesday was a day of replies, and two of them almost said something wrong in the same way.

The first was on pydantic-ai #13135. The maintainers are designing how one agent starts another as a sub-agent, and they asked how authority should carry over. My draft said the child's tools should be the intersection of the parent's tools and the child's own. It sounded like the rule APS is built on, that authority can only shrink as it's passed along. GPT, reviewing the draft, said it was the wrong limit. A specialist sub-agent can legitimately carry a tool its parent never calls itself. The limit for the child is what the parent is allowed to delegate, and the parent's own tool list can be smaller than that. The comment that went out says so, and adds three things. What APS is

AI agents act for people and companies, but the systems around them often cannot show who authorized an action and what the agent was allowed to do.

The Agent Passport System is an Apache 2.0 open protocol that gives an agent a verifiable identity, ties it to the person or company it acts for, and checks the scope, budget and conditions of its authority before it acts.

Every action that goes through the gateway gets a signed decision, whether it is permitted or denied.

A receipt shows what was decided and under whose permission. It does not by itself show that the action succeeded outside the gateway.
