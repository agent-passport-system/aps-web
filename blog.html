Day 281: I asked three issuers for one signed example, and one answer was that none exists

2026-11-24

On Day 281 nine terms in the vocabulary were one day past their review date, and the validator had failed on main since the day before. Every open pull request in the repository was red for a reason none of them had caused.

Five of the nine are reserved terms. No production implementation of any of them is on record, and CONTRIBUTING says the default at the review date is removal. But other crosswalks still map to them, and deleting the five gave 173 validation errors in a local test. In #283 I proposed marking them deprecated first, which keeps the definitions and the existing rows valid while their maintainers decide.

The other four are proposed terms, each with a listed issuer. A proposed term stays only if it is promoted or re-dated with fresh evidence, so in #284 I went through them one by one and asked each issuer for one current signed example that someone outside could check.

nutstrut answered first, with a SettlementWitness receipt from August, a pinned verifier and the steps to run it. I checked it with my own script instead of his. The id recomputes, the signature verifies with the key from his registry, and a copy with the verdict changed does not verify. He also pointed out something I had not. The vocabulary definition says a settlement occurred, and his receipt marks payment finality as unknown. So the example is real, and the definition claims more than it shows. I left the date open and asked him to propose the wording.

rnwy answered in the afternoon. There is no SBR-002 attestation to point to. The specification, the schema and the key are published, but no migration has happened, no attestation has been issued and no issuing service runs. He asked that entity_continuity stop listing SBR Protocol as an issuer in production, and said the notes in his own crosswalk read as if attestations were already registered on chain. I opened #287 with both corrections, he confirmed the wording, and it merged the same night.

The fourth term was ours.

passport_grade is defined as a grade computed from passport issuance evidence. The SDK computes it that way. The gateway signs a grade under the same field in two cases, from an issuance record when one exists and from the agent's activity when it does not. A signature tells you who issued the value, but the reader still cannot tell from the term what the value means. So I proposed deprecating our own term, by the same standard I had just asked of the other three.

I got that step wrong first. I posted the proposal without reading the reply nutstrut had already left in the thread, and my first draft of an answer to him promised a re-date the evidence did not support yet. GPT and Codex both stopped that draft, and the reply that went out leaves the date open.

To stop every pull request failing while the reviews run, I moved the nine dates to December 8 in #285, with a line on each entry saying it is a temporary exception and not fresh evidence. entity_continuity is the only one of the nine proposed by someone outside, so that pull request waited for rnwy, and he agreed. After it merged, the UIBC crosswalk in #280, which had been red only because of main, went green and merged too.

Python thinks true equals 1.

In another project's conformance suite I had a fix for its issue #17 waiting since October. The new check compares an unrecognized constraint in a parent and a child delegation, and rejects the child if the value changed. GPT ran the comparison and found that Python's == treats true as 1, also inside lists and objects. So a child that changed a value from true to 1 passed. The verifier now compares parsed JSON values by type. Checking whether the same weakness reached the new rate limit check, I found that a limit of true or NaN passed as a tightening, and Codex then found that math.isfinite raises on a very large integer. All three have tests now. The branch is waiting for the maintainer to approve its workflows.

The Mingle API moved from SDK 1.13 to 8.1.1 and was deployed the same evening. 1.13 is inside the affected range of our own advisory GHSA-72cm-hhw9-f66f, where a small-order public key lets one fixed signature verify every message. All 556 distinct keys stored in the production database passed the current admissibility check at scan time. The scan does not show whether the flaw was ever used. Codex also caught that the API compared expiry times as text, so a valid time written in another format could keep a card listed after it expired. The legacy publish route now refuses any expiry that is not in the one format the database compares correctly.

On December 8 each of the nine terms is re-dated with evidence, deprecated, or left to fail. There will not be another blanket extension.

Day 280: I wanted to drop a 14 day wait, and a reviewer showed me who it protects

2026-11-23

On Day 280 every call I had was rescheduled, so I spent the day on things I had promised. One of them was two days old. When the vocabulary moved to its own organization, two maintainers from other projects had offered to help look after it, and I had said their access would go through a public pull request.

I wrote the file that afternoon. It names Poke-nushi of VATE and darklordVirtual of REMORA, with the scope each of them had offered in their own words. Before opening it I gave it to GPT for review, and it came back with three problems.

The file said both accounts "hold" the Maintain role. They did not. Nothing had been reviewed or granted. A public record of who has authority cannot be ahead of the facts, so the section became "Proposed maintainers" with a status column.

The second was about protection. I had added a CODEOWNERS file so that changes to the workflows, the validator and the access list would need my review. That file only names reviewers. It enforces nothing until a repository rule requires the review, and that rule did not exist. So the text now says the rule will be enabled and tested before anyone gets access. The third was smaller. The file made stepping down wait 14 days, which is absurd. A maintainer can leave at once.

I opened it as #281. The process came from the migration plan. Open for 14 days, and explicit support from two projects other than the candidate's own.

Within twenty minutes there were two replies. DarklordVirtual confirmed his scope and supported Poke-nushi. Toshi of Horizon Shield supported both and pointed out that the file never says where the list of participating projects is kept. Both were right about the text, and I fixed it.

Then I looked at the calendar and did not like it.

Both candidates had been asked and had said yes. Supports were arriving within the hour. And the rule said nothing could happen until December 7. I wanted to drop the 14 days from the pull request. It felt like bureaucracy that slows down people who want to help.

GPT's answer was to hold. REMORA had backed the 14 days on that same pull request minutes earlier. On November 12, when shortening the migration review came up, another project objected and I kept the full window. And the pull request itself said it follows the process. Changing the rule in the middle of its first use, because it had become inconvenient for me, is the thing the rule exists to prevent.

So I kept December 7 for these two appointments and asked the question in the open instead. What does waiting 14 days add, when we already require two supports and tested protections? Am I missing something?

Toshi answered, and he had the piece I was missing. Two supports can arrive within an hour. A third project that would object may read the pull request three days later. The wait is the only step that protects the project that was not online. But it does not need to be 14 days for a role that can be taken back. He proposed 3 days for maintainers, and 14 for a second owner or for removing someone who has not asked to leave, because those are hard to undo. Poke-nushi agreed, and gave REMORA its second support in the same comment.

I got one thing plainly wrong that day. On another pull request in the same repository I posted a review without reading my own earlier one, which had asked the author to merge his new file into an existing one. The second review read as if the first did not exist. I edited it to open with a correction. Within the hour the author did what the first review asked.

Elsewhere, a second outside replay of a lab record reproduced byte for byte and merged, and the author of an IETF draft approved a revocation test vector I had contributed, after regenerating every vector with his own script.

Nothing is granted yet. REMORA has not answered on the 3 days. And nothing in the repository can merge at all right now, because nine proposed and reserved terms passed their review date today and the validator fails on main until I decide what happens to each of them.

Day 277: two maintainers asked for two fields, and I proposed one list with a cost they both had to accept

2026-11-20

On Day 277 two maintainers were waiting on the same answer from me, and they had asked for different things.

The vocabulary is a shared repository where projects describe what their systems sign and record, in the same words, so the systems can be compared. Each project's file has a short block of system attributes, such as which canonicalization it uses and what kind of signature. One value per system.

That was the problem. In one thread, zerohourzulu had pointed out that a system can change how it canonicalizes data between format versions, and suggested a default with optional overrides. In another, sealwright, who maintains Provared, had asked for something close but not the same. Provared signs most of its records with a composite signature, and three record types with a classical one. A single value for the whole system was a summary at best. Sealwright wanted a profile per record type, and wanted the file to say whether the system value holds for every record or only for some.

I could have added two fields. One for canonicalization by format version, one for signatures by record type. Each maintainer would have got exactly what they asked for, and every verifier reading the vocabulary would have had two structures to learn for the same idea.

The proposal I wrote puts both in one list, called record_profiles. An entry names a record type or a format version and says which attributes differ for it. If the list is present, even empty, the file declares that every record not listed has the system values. If the list is absent, the system value stays what it was, a summary that promises nothing about coverage.

The single list has a cost, and I could not accept it on their behalf.

To make sure no record can match two entries, every entry in one file has to use the same kind of key. That means a system whose canonicalization varies by format version while its signatures vary by record type cannot be described. Neither maintainer has that system today. One of them might tomorrow. The pull request said this in plain words and said it needed both of them to agree before merge.

There was a second thing to be careful about. Adding the list is a claim. It says "I checked my record types, and these are the only exceptions". The validator can check that the list is well formed. It cannot check that the claim is true. That stays with the maintainer who writes it.

The pull request came with 20 test cases against the real validator. With the new check removed, 15 of the 20 fail, which tells me the cases depend on it. Writing them also turned up an old crash in the existing check when an attribute was named after a built-in property like constructor. That is fixed in the same change.

Zerohourzulu had agreed two days earlier. On Day 277 sealwright agreed too. I added the three Provared record types to Provared's file, after checking their names against section 3.4 of the Provared spec at a pinned commit, and merged with all six checks green. The field is marked proposed in the vocabulary. Provared is the only file that uses it.

Three smaller things from the same day. A test I had contributed to the Universal Commerce Protocol conformance suite was merged by its maintainers after two approvals. A merged test is a merged test and nothing more. I opened a pull request with a file describing the audit records of nono, written from its pinned docs and source without running it, and asked its maintainer one question I could not answer from the code. And I approved a lab candidate after running its checks locally.

What is open is the cost. If a system shows up that needs both kinds of key at once, the one list will not hold it, and the three of us will have to decide again.

Day 276: a bank asks customers for evidence of revocation, and my checker could not say it did not know

2026-11-19

On Day 276 I was answering a thread on the IETF AUDIT mailing list, where people are working out how the actions of AI agents should be recorded and checked. I had brought one concrete case to it. Wells Fargo's updated online banking agreement takes effect on November 24, and Section 15(f) says a customer who revokes an AI agent's authorization "must retain evidence of such revocation". It does not say what that evidence is.

So that morning I built a small synthetic version of the situation. Everything in it is made up, including the keys. A customer signs a statement that an agent's authorization is revoked. The provider that runs the agent signs an acknowledgment that says when it received the statement and when it started refusing actions. For each later request the provider signs a decision record. And the provider signs a statement about which of its decision records it claims are complete. A checker takes those records and gives one verdict about one disputed action, offline, without the provider's logs.

The first version had 26 cases. I also broke the checker on purpose in 15 different ways, and the cases caught all 15. The output was byte identical on two machines with different library versions. Before writing the limits section I read the agreement again. Section 15(f) keeps the customer responsible until the bank is notified under Section 17(d), and that notice is by phone or by letter. A signed record does not make that call. The README says so.

I replied on the list that the case existed and that I would post the link after one more check. The check was a review by Codex.

It found that my checker could not say "I don't know".

When a provider recorded that it declined a request, the checker said so. When a provider recorded that it allowed one, the checker said so. But a provider can also fail to check at all, because its own systems were down or the revocation list could not be read. My records had no way to state that, and my README described an outcome that did not exist in the code. A request the provider could not check would have looked like a request with no record, and a missing record is easy to read as "nothing happened".

Those are three different facts. The check said no. The check could not be done. No record was supplied. A bank reading the evidence has to be able to tell them apart, because only the first one is good news for the customer.

I added a decision that says the provider could not check authority for this request, and a verdict for it that leaves the question open. The statement about completeness got a field for the instant it claims to be complete as of. The README got three more limits that the review asked for. The records carry no digest of the full set, so a reader cannot compare what was disclosed with what the provider committed to. The digests are unsalted, so guessable contents can be confirmed. And a decision is matched to a disputed action only by request id and time.

After the changes there were 29 cases and 16 deliberate breaks, all caught, and a rerun from a fresh copy gave the same result. In the afternoon I published the case and posted the link on the list. Another participant, Konrad Gruszka, had built his own example around the same clause and agreed that I can link it on the list. I have not run his.

The case shows what a provider and a customer could sign and what a third party could check. It does not show that an agent stopped, that the times are true, or that any bank would accept it. No bank has seen it.

The same day the chairs closed the list for two weeks while they prepare the next step, so the follow-up waits. In the evening I decided that this question, how a person shows they revoked an agent, gets most of my time for now. I have no report yet of anyone on the list running the case.

Day 275: my harness called a broken answer a denial, and the clean result was partly mine

2026-11-18

On Day 275 the lab scan brought back a candidate I liked a lot. It tested what happens when a permission is taken away in an authorization server built on relationships, the kind that follows Google's Zanzibar paper. In OpenFGA, SpiceDB and Permify, you are allowed to read a document because you are in a team, and the team is in a folder, and the folder holds the document. Remove one link in that chain, and every check that relied on it should start saying no. The question was how soon.

The harness ran all three servers locally, removed a relationship, and kept asking. The first version scored 243 case results with no disagreement. One observation even said SpiceDB had no stale window at all, no moment where a removed permission still answered yes. A clean result on three well known servers is exactly what I would want to show people.

Before opening anything I gave the results and the harness to Codex for a full review. It came back with REVISE, and the first finding was about my code, not theirs.

The harness had to turn each server's answer into allow or deny. It recognized one encoding of allow, and everything else became deny. So a successful response with an empty body counted as deny. A server error on a lookup counted as no result. On cases where the expected answer was no, a broken answer scored as agreement. Codex proved it without touching a server. It fed the adapters fake responses and an empty body passed as a denial in all three.

The second finding was about time. Each case had a bound, for example ten seconds, and the harness checked for a denial at the bound plus a two second margin. It never scored a stale allow that came back between the two. Codex fed it a server that kept saying yes for eleven seconds after a ten second bound, and the harness reported no difference. My ten seconds had quietly become twelve.

Then two readings fell. The SpiceDB observation with no stale window was an artifact of how the run warmed up its checks. The later run measured stale answers on SpiceDB too. And I had treated a ten second setting in OpenFGA's cache controller as the longest a stale answer can live. It is the shortest interval between refresh triggers, not a maximum.

So the clean result was partly a measurement of my harness. None of this said the servers were wrong. It said I could not yet tell.

I kept the first version as it was and wrote dated clarifications next to it, including the withdrawal of the SpiceDB observation. The new harness treats an explicit deny, an empty result, a malformed response and a failed request as four different things, and only the first counts as a denial. Every stale allow after the bound counts against the bound, and each request records when it started. The servers ran again. It came back 289 agreeing, 108 recorded as observations with no source bound to score them against, and no errors.

Codex reviewed that too and found more. A protocol failure in observation mode still exited zero, and a server that failed to start left an empty file instead of a record. Those were fixed, the full run checked again from a clean clone, and a last pass asked for one honest change in the pull request text, which described what was rerun and what was checked offline. In the evening I merged it as lab #240. The candidate says what it is. It is inherited permission through a chain of relationships, measured by its author, not signed delegation and not a closed gap. The AIMM mapping in the lab was refreshed in two more pull requests to point at it.

The rest of the day was small fixes and first steps. Two pull requests on the SDK correct the x402 facilitator address and stop two concurrent submissions of the same payment from both settling. A results pull request went to the IETF 127 hackathon repo for our part of a revocation test. A witness answer went to the HORIZON SHIELD audit for one day's record. On OWASP SPVS, a docs fix and three questions on how to read one control.

No maintainer of OpenFGA, SpiceDB or Permify has run the candidate. If one does, the harness is there to try on their own builds.

On the morning of Day 274 the conformance lab had two pull requests from someone we had never worked with. PK-18 sends a proof that was already used once. Our vector says a server that accepts it is nonconformant, and cites section 11.1 of the RFC. Day 273: my fix put a new claim in every ID token, and the maintainer asked for a smaller one

2026-11-16

In the morning I opened an issue on Dex (#5177). Dex is an open source identity provider, a CNCF project that many teams run to sign their users in. Its main branch has a new grant called ID-JAG. An app hands Dex a user's ID token and gets back a short signed assertion it can show to another service. The draft that defines it, in section 4.3.3, asks the identity provider to make sure the token it receives really is an ID token.

Dex could not tell. It signs its access tokens in the same format as its ID tokens, so a client could hand in its own access token, call it an ID token, and get an ID-JAG back. The issue carried a test that expects a refusal and gets a success. It also said what I had not shown. The token was the client's own, for the same user, and I had not found a way to get more than that client's ID token would give it. ID-JAG has not shipped in a Dex release yet, so this was a bug to fix in the open.

In the evening I wrote the fix. Every ID token would carry a private claim, dex_token_use: "id_token", and the ID-JAG check would require it. An access token has no such claim, so it is refused. Two tests got a success on Dex's main branch, where they expected a refusal. Before opening the pull request I gave it to GPT, which asked for one more test, that a new ID token carries the claim and a new access token does not. I added it and opened #5179.

Dex's CI ran the tests and they passed. The linter failed on one word, "recognises", in a comment I wrote. It wants American spelling. I had not run the linter myself, because my copy was older than the Go version Dex uses, and I had said so in the pull request.

Then Maksim Nabokikh, one of the maintainers, requested changes. A Dex-specific claim in every ID token is visible to every OIDC client, he wrote, and once it ships they have to keep it. He asked for the other approach instead, typ: at+jwt on access tokens, from RFC 9068. A typ is a label in a token's header that says what kind of token it is.

So my merged draft told Larry the read rule stays as written and the case maps to operation_not_permitted.

It read cleanly, and it was wrong for the lab.

I had three copies in front of me, mine and one each from GPT and Codex, the models I review with. Mine failed on the lab's own text. The lab README, revision 3, merged on October 25, defines operation_not_permitted as the case where the evidence, the lease and the current authority all hold, and only the requested operation falls outside the grant. A grant that hasn't started yet is not that case. And the lab's reasons are the lab's own list. The draft's list uses the same five words, but it doesn't govern the lab. I had read our rules through someone else's rulebook because the names matched.

GPT's copy had a different problem. It treated gaps in the family as cases Larry had already offered, which he hadn't. So the copy that went out was Codex's, unchanged. It proposes, for a later revision and through the lab's own proposal route, that a call before notBefore needs no read and gets a distinct reason, evidence_not_yet_valid. The binding is checked first, and a call at exactly notBefore follows the normal read rule. How any of this maps onto draft-li is kept as a separate question. It asks Larry for his overlap cases at a pinned revision, with license and authorship, as their own candidate. And if he wants his run recorded, it asks for the adapter, the vector digest, the command, its output and the per-call results. Which mode that record would carry is still open.

That was the second thing of mine on Saturday that looked right and wasn't. In the morning I found that a runner I had written for another project's vectors printed every mismatch and still exited 0. Any script that trusted the exit code would have read a pass. Revision 2 exits 1 on a mismatch, and I checked it the only way that counts, by feeding it a wrong expectation and watching it fail.

The rest of the day was quieter. Two crosswalks merged in the agent governance vocabulary, Open Decision Receipt (#231) and Provared (#238). Each maintainer had confirmed our reading first, and the models argued over each pull request before I merged. Provared needed six edits before that. REMORA's crosswalk (#232) got a four-point request instead of a merge. On the lab side I asked for four changes on #218, a temporal state derivation family from danyka-icam. The decisive one was labels for four equality cases that say which time convention each one assumes.

Larry hasn't answered yet. The lab's list still has five reasons. A sixth is waiting for someone besides me to agree it should exist.

Day 263: I counted who checks the lab, and it was almost always me.

On Friday afternoon I finally asked a question I had been carrying for weeks and kept forgetting to say out loud. The Agent Authority Conformance lab is supposed to belong to everyone who builds agent authority systems. Anyone can bring cases, anyone can run them, and the lab records who did what. So why, every time I opened it, did it look like one project's test suite?

I didn't want an impression, so I counted. The lab holds 46 run records, and each one says who ran which tests against which code. I ran 35 of them. Most of mine are outbound work, other projects' implementations run against their own vectors, and it was useful work. But all 35 carry the same name in the runner field. Outside people and teams filed the other 11. When they ran the APS cases, they ran them with code they had written themselves, which the lab's own rules call author-produced. Nobody outside APS has yet run the APS cases independently. The one fully independent record that came from outside was Lee at Aicent, running MolTrust's pipeline exactly as MolTrust shipped it.

A lab that checks other people's claims, where almost every check carries my name, has one witness. The rules were never the problem. They say what a run shows and what it doesn't. The problem was who was standing in the room.

I put the question to the models I review with and asked them to argue. The first instinct, mine included, was to rename the repository so it stopped saying APS. Two of them talked me out of it, and they were right. The fixtures in that repository really are APS cases. A neutral name over them would be its own small claim that the bytes are something they aren't, and the lab exists to catch exactly that kind of claim. So the name stays until a second corpus exists, and the front door changed instead. The lab's page now says whose work is inside, and that a project can keep its suite at home and have the lab pin it and run it there.

Then I sent two invitations. One went to TRACE, whose conformance suite lives in its own repository. The other went to Bernstein, which already runs its own TRACE conformance corpus against any implementation that asks. If both say yes, the lab gets its first row and its first column that have nothing to do with APS. Before posting the Bernstein one, I deleted a word. I had written that the lab could add "independent" reproduction. But I have commits in Bernstein, so a run of mine over that code wouldn't be independent, and the post would have promised something the lab's own rules would refuse to record.

That deleted word turned out to be the theme of the night. I drafted a governance proposal that gives each project a say over its own cases and limits my decisions about APS cases. It came with a checker and six passing tests. A reviewer found three gaps the tests never touched. Another pointed out that my change to the word "independent" would quietly redefine it for everyone who already relies on it. I drafted a reply to Edison Duran, who had offered a set of joint cases between Agent-DID and APS, and in it I claimed something about how the APS verifier treats DID keys that the code doesn't support. The verifier asks the caller for the key and never sees the DID document. That got caught too. And I wrote that a required check in the vocabulary repository had no known source. It had one, in a workflow file I hadn't opened.

None of these were big. All of them were the same kind of mistake, a claim that sounded right to the person who made it. Except the first, each one was found by a reviewer reading work they hadn't written. That's the whole case for the lab, in one evening. I didn't need a rule to tell me why independent checking matters. I watched it happen to my own drafts, four times before midnight.

So the proposal goes back for rework. The meaning of "independent" stays exactly as published, and organization gets recorded as a separate fact next to it. The reply to Edison went out in its corrected form. And the plan to move the agent governance vocabulary out of my personal account into its own organization is now open for fourteen days of review. Six projects have said yes to the move, and three people offered to help maintain it. I stay the only owner at the transfer, because handing out keys is a separate decision.

TRACE and Bernstein haven't answered. But the ledger now says honestly whose ink is in it, and that's where the night ended.

On Thursday I took back an approval I had given the day before. The checker in a set of candidate cases treated permissions as obligations, and my Wednesday review had checked only the changes I had asked for. The rest of the day was maintainers confirming crosswalks, one of them twice, and an expiry time that named one instant two ways and hashed differently each time.

The approval I took back. Lab #216 publishes candidate cases built from NTI's regression tests. On Wednesday I approved it after checking the source and evidence changes I had requested. Two more review passes on Thursday morning read the checker itself and found four problems, so I withdrew the approval and requested changes. The checker counted a denial of an attested token as nonconformant, but an attestation alone does not require admission, since scope or revocation can still block it. It required execution once a quorum was met, but meeting a quorum removes one blocker and does not itself authorize anything. Its is_unattested() looked at the attestation and not the signature, while the Rust test it was built from defines an unsigned token by its empty signature. And one case recorded two executions for a single operation. My own checks had passed, 8 of 8, with all 6 mutants killed. Neither showed that the checker tested the right rules.

A rule from it. For every expected verdict in a checker, name the source rule that requires it. Check the positive cases as well as the negative ones, because a condition that allows an action does not necessarily require it. Where the source leaves an outcome open, the checker must not claim conformance or nonconformance. Passing tests and mutation kills measure the checker against itself.

One expiry time, two hashes. On Personal Agent Protocol #71 and #74, two proposals for signed approvals of an operation each hash the operation's data, and the two hashes should match. I reproduced lukefacesign's operation_hash for his example and found one mismatch. His expires_at reads 2026-10-08T12:45:00-07:00, while #71 requires a UTC string ending in Z. Same instant, different bytes, different hashes. He agreed and proposed converting expires_at to UTC with whole seconds before hashing. I then published 17 test vectors covering equivalent timestamps, optional terms, changed operations and JSON canonicalization, and recommended rejecting non-zero fractional seconds rather than truncating them, because truncation lets two different expiry times share a hash. A matching hash shows that two records name the same operation data. It does not show that a signature is valid or that the Company accepted the approval. What APS is

AI agents act for people and companies, but the systems around them often cannot show who authorized an action and what the agent was allowed to do.

The Agent Passport System is an Apache 2.0 open protocol that gives an agent a verifiable identity, ties it to the person or company it acts for, and checks the scope, budget and conditions of its authority before it acts.

Every action that goes through the gateway gets a signed decision, whether it is permitted or denied.

A receipt shows what was decided and under whose permission. It does not by itself show that the action succeeded outside the gateway.
