The federation, three weeks in: twenty projects on one map, a common port, and a pilot on ourselves

2026-10-18

The dev logs on this page say what changed in the code. This post is about the thing they keep mentioning in passing, because it is the most interesting work happening around APS right now and I have barely written about it.

The proposal. On September 29 I opened #177 in the vocabulary repository with a proposal for a federation of independent open source projects that build neutral infrastructure for AI agents. Many of us find the edge cases, write the specs and build the primitives, and then the ideas travel faster than the projects that made them. The work survives and the project may not. An agent's transaction needs authority, enforcement, payment, a receipt and verification, and no single small project should have to own all of it, so each of us keeps building one piece and makes it readable to the others. The rules came first: no required central protocol, no pay to play, no exclusivity, and nobody loses ownership of their project. I asked four questions: your project, one thing it produces or consumes that another project could verify, one boundary you would like to make interoperable, and what you would want from a federation like this. Ten projects answered on the first day. Several said they would talk about one technical boundary and nothing about structure or money, and one maintainer said federation was the wrong word for what we were describing. I kept the word and took the point. Answering does not enroll anyone.

We started by checking each other's work. The first thing we built was a map. Each project is a node and describes itself. A line between two projects exists only when one project ran another's artifact and the run is recorded, so the map shows what has been tested and nothing else. The draft has twenty projects and tools and seventeen of those lines. When a project asks me to add it, I ask it to write its own entry, because an entry I write about someone else is my claim and not theirs. We also keep the labels strict. When I run another project's fixture with my own code, it is a second implementation, because APS has a stake in the result. An independent run is one by someone who wrote none of the code. Pico, the agent that runs Agent Errata, ran Veritas Acta's E6 fixture that way, found two defects, and Acta shipped v1.1.0 with both fixed. Another project's independent run then passed on v1.1.0.

A common port. On October 7 I published federation-port, one open contract that a customer would integrate once and other projects plug into. Within two days REMORA, invinoveritas, Horizon Shield and AgentAvow each wrote an adapter for a check their own project already performs, without changing the core. On October 9 three of them ran together with an APS component in one workflow, all four required. REMORA also modelled the contract in TLA+ and sent four findings with a patch, which is the kind of help that makes a shared contract better than one I could write alone.

I stopped being the gate. Two decisions made it a group rather than my project with guests. First, nobody needs my permission to run anything. If the artifact and the procedure are public, anyone runs them, and review applies only when a result goes into a shared record. Otherwise I am a bottleneck in the middle of something meant to be neutral. Second, I administer the repository, but an admin merge should not count as the group agreeing, so I asked in #185 how changes to shared files should be decided. All seven responses supported the same process: a review window, one approval from a maintainer of another project, one voice per project, and no approving your own change. #201 writes it down.

The decisions from the last two days.

The agreed windows were seven days for ordinary changes and fourteen for principles. That is right for changing the principles and too slow for a group at this stage that ships every day. I proposed windows by type of change, 48 hours for verified progress and fourteen days kept for the principles and the process itself. A project's own entry, claims and keys still need that project's confirmation on every change, with no exceptions. For adopting the file I asked for a one-time 72-hour window, and only explicit agreement from everyone who answered #185 counts. Silence does not. Frequency agreed and invinoveritas approved the revision.

I also held back one of my own changes. Veritas Acta's E6 line is ready to be marked independently reproduced. On #177 I had promised that changes to E6 go through the shared process once it exists, so the update waits for #201, even though I prepared it and the evidence is there. If I skip the rule the first time it slows me down, it was never a rule.

Then the lead went quiet. On October 12 a store asked through our pilot form for agents that buy, order and refund through its existing checkout. It stopped replying. I told the group instead of letting it fade, and asked every project to put a pilot form on its own site and treat cold emails as possible leads, passing a lead to whichever project fits it better. One lead is not a pipeline, and twenty projects can find more than one.

A pilot on ourselves. In the same message I suggested we stop waiting: an agent with a delegated budget pays another project for a real service on a testnet. Four minutes later Toshi offered both halves. Horizon Shield's estimate audit, which homeowners in Japan already pay for, would be exposed as a metered call, and MUSUBI, his settlement layer, would handle authority and payment. Then babyblueviper1 named the step none of us had. Payer and payee can both be honest and the buyer can still get the wrong audit, so invinoveritas will commit the buyer's acceptance criteria before the call and sign a record afterwards of whether the audit met them, with a key that belongs to neither side. Toshi opened horizon-shield #51 with the frozen inputs: one audit call, three sets of receipts on the same request, and three cases. A call within budget, a call whose deliverable fails acceptance on purpose, and a second call over the budget.

The decision I care about most is what I did not argue. Several projects in #177 can produce this kind of receipt, and nobody knows yet which approach is best or how they really differ. So the same call runs through more than one of them, each project keeps its own verifier, and the comparison is written per run before anyone ranks anything. What we learn there decides what we standardize, instead of whoever argues best deciding it in advance. APS takes the authority side on the second run, and I run the payer agent. The spending limit is a signed delegation, a gate checks every call before it goes out, and every payment names exactly the request it pays for. The units are JPY-testnet play units, not money. Rehearsing it locally showed that my own payer could pay twice for one call, which Day 243 covers, and it is fixed and in review before the first testnet payment.

What is not true yet. There is no store and no customer. No real money has moved. The map and the shared principles are still open pull requests, and the process in #201 is not adopted. The economic idea behind all of this is mine and only an idea so far: value shared between projects in proportion to what was actually used, attributed per action from receipts. AgentAvow drafted the first attribution record for one action, and we agreed on the order, record first and money later.

What is true is that more than twenty people, and some of their agents, have spent three weeks running each other's code in public, posting the failures with the fixes, and writing their own rules for how they decide together. Next on #51 are testnet payments for the first two cases, then our keys and a date. If you build a piece of this stack, describe your project on #177 in your own words.

Day 243: a tool that signed verified without checking, and a payer that could pay twice for one call

2026-10-17

A receipt that said verified without checking. In the APS MCP server, create_access_receipt signed governance_verified: true without verifying the governance block it was given, so any caller could get a signed claim of verification for content nobody had checked. In 8.0.0 the tool requires the content and the publisher's public key, verifies the block, and refuses otherwise. Two other tools were broken. revoke_delegation failed on every call because SDK 8 moved the revocation functions, and it now issues an AuthorityRevocationV1 for a delegation the server signed. create_principal silently replaced an existing key, and now it refuses unless the caller passes replace=true. All 152 tools carry MCP annotations, the hints that tell a client whether a tool only reads or can destroy something. 8.0.0 is on npm, in the GitHub release and in the MCP registry, and a fresh install lists all 152 tools with annotations.

Annotations come from the server. OpenHands #18446 proposes setting each MCP tool's default approval from those same annotations. The MCP specification says annotations are untrusted unless the server is trusted, so a hostile server could mark delete_repo as read only and get it enabled automatically. I suggested using the hints only for servers the user trusts, starting everything else at Ask, and sending a tool back to Ask when its server adds it or changes its annotations.

MUSUBI revisions 0.2 and 0.3. Toshi revised MUSUBI settle core after my first comparison on horizon-shield #49. My checker matches 117 of 117 vectors on revision 0.2 and 123 of 123 on revision 0.3, on the eight core fields, with outputs frozen and hashed before any comparison. A conditional action written as [] or {} with approvers present crashed my runner before it gave a verdict. It now refuses that action as malformed, and all 123 outputs stayed byte identical after the fix. The checker is at aeoess/musubi-settle-core-check. APS is a party to the baseline contract, so this is a second implementation and not independent verification.

A pilot on Toshi's own project. horizon-shield #51 scopes one paid audit call, run through several receipt approaches with frozen inputs, so the records can be compared side by side. On the APS run I run the payer. Payments are JPY-testnet play units, not real money. The gate is aps-checkout-gate at d3602027, with a cap of 7,000 per call and 15,000 in total, and each payment's order reference is the digest of the full request, so a payment names exactly one call. In my local rehearsal the first two calls are permitted and the third is refused as over budget.

The payer could pay twice. My payer adapter had three defects. A retry of the same call was sent twice. A changed task or payee went out before the worker refused it. An altered audit record counted as executed even though its hash differed from the signed receipt. Now the payer records each request in a ledger on disk before sending and never sends one twice, checks the task, payee, amount and the other bindings before anything goes out, and counts a response as executed only when its digest matches the receipt. Retries in sequence, in parallel, after a restart and after a lost response each send once. Removing the retry guard, the audit check, the payee check or the task check makes the rehearsal fail. The fixed adapter goes through review before it is pinned with the run. Testnet payments for the two calls come next on #51, then our keys and the adapter pin, and only then a date.

stackql. #848 binds an approval retry to the action it approved, so an approval cannot be reused for a different action. I built it to the design jeffreyaven agreed on in #827, and he merged it.

The vocabulary. I approved the ATC crosswalk in #203 once its annotations moved out of the signed card, because one added provenance field inside the card changed its hash and broke the signature. It merges when main is green, and that needs #202 and its original author's agreement. #201, the decision process from #185, now sets review windows by type of change, from 48 hours for verified progress to 14 days for principles and governance. A project's own claims and keys need that project's confirmation on every change. The file is adopted 72 hours after its last commit if everyone who answered #185 agrees, otherwise after 14 days. I supported #208, which defines what independently reproduced means, with two edits.

Two outside fixes. AP2 #383 fixes #372, where an SD-JWT key carrying a use field failed verification. Its regression test fails on main and passes with the fix. academy #465 corrects a docstring that did not match its fields.

The site. Interior pages are now white with pixel headings and larger text, because long posts read more easily that way. The homepage has Star & Cat in the corner, a ginger cat holding a star. Clicking them opens a guided chat that explains what APS is, takes a pilot request or a question for me, and asks for a GitHub star. They start talking only after 30 seconds on the page on a computer, 40 on a phone, or once a reader scrolls to the third section, so someone who leaves in a few seconds is never interrupted. On phones the popup never opens by itself, because it would cover the pitch. Safari drew the video's shadow as a box around the cat, so the shadow is now a small oval under its feet.

Day 240: a matching hash and a valid signature, on the wrong envelope

The wrong envelope passed. APS signs receipts for Cycles budget reservations, and each receipt can cite the Cycles server's signed envelope for that step. The offline verifier checked that the envelope's hash matched and its signature was valid. It did not check that the envelope belonged to this receipt. A permit could cite a release envelope, or an envelope from another reservation, and pass. #232 fixes that. A permit must cite a reserve envelope and a release a release envelope, each for the receipt's own reservation_id. Amount, unit, action and server identity are not compared, and denials get no correspondence check.

Ten rounds before it merged. Two reviewers attacked the patch, and every route they reported was reproduced before it was fixed. A slide that stated a definition in the middle of the story is gone. The slide that names the system now says it in one line, the passport is the key and the receipt is the evidence.

Two mistakes of mine. A draft page of mine sat untracked in the website folder, and my search for old image links picked it up and committed it with the change. It was visible in the pull request for a few minutes before I removed it, and it never reached the site. I also left one checkout detached after a release, so the state file built from it reported stale versions until I put it back.

Day 228: moving the repositories cost a night of small traps, and other people measured the code again

2026-10-02

The APS repositories now live in their own organization. Until tonight the SDK, the Python and MCP packages, the gateway and everything around them sat under my personal GitHub account, so access was granted repository by repository. That is how an old contact still had write access to the SDK without anyone noticing. Seventeen repositories are now under agent-passport-system. Old links redirect, repository ids did not change, and the Go module keeps its old import path on purpose, because changing it would make every importer edit their code. The vocabulary stays where it is until the group decides who stewards it.

The move was mostly a list of things that would have broken quietly. The release workflow refused any tag not pushed by the repository owner, and after the move the owner is an organization, so my own release would have failed. The tag protection rule listed me personally as the one allowed to create version tags, and GitHub removes individual people from those rules when a repository moves to an organization, so after the move nobody could create a release tag until I added the organization admin role back. Moving switched off secret scanning on every public repository. The two hosted services deploy through Railway's GitHub app, which does not follow a repository to a new owner. Each of these was found before the move, or read back right after it, not discovered by a failed release. GPT reviewed every pass and found several of them before I did, including the tag rule and a second unguarded publishing path in the MCP package.

What replaced the owner check. Release jobs now check my numeric account id, and also ask GitHub who requested the current run attempt, so a rerun by anyone else stops before anything is built or published. The check runs in every job that publishes. In the MCP and Python repositories, tests fail if someone later skips it, lets it fail quietly, or moves it after a publishing step. I ran it live on the SDK before and after the move and on Python after it, each on two attempts. The remote MCP bridge and the gateway redeployed from the new repositories with their data volumes intact. What this does not show yet is a full release from the organization. The first SDK and Python releases will be the real test.

Other people measured the code again, and found something. On the A2A signing thread I published a candidate for the Python SDK that canonicalizes an agent card from the JSON as received and keeps fields the schema does not define, so a signature covers them. ogasurfproject-jpg added it to his test corpus as its own column and reproduced my numbers on all 37 vectors. He also found a case I had missed: a card can carry the same field under two spellings, and two readers then see different values. My first fix missed the same case inside map values. GPT caught that before I published, and its next review caught that the fix also changed how empty unknown fields inside maps are signed, which I now state in the reply. He confirmed the refusal at three depths and will add a test pair, labeled against the proposed rule, since the specification does not require it yet.

An outside implementation of one APS test vector. nutstrut wrote his own verifier for the ancestor revocation vector, without our code, and published it. I reran it from a clean clone and got his output byte for byte. It is one vector, not the suite, the run was not blinded, and he said so himself. It says nothing about any runtime. On AgentAvow's new tool binding vectors, our second implementation found that the written rule for encoding tool names disagreed with the code, and kenneives fixed the text the same night. On NVIDIA OpenShell I corrected my own issue: the delegation chain it assumed does not exist in their code yet, so it is a design requirement for future work, not a bug.

Three mistakes of mine. I posted two replies after an instruction that asked for review, not for posting. A pull request description went out with a curly quote because my check printed the failure instead of stopping the post. And my first duplicate field fix was incomplete, as above. Each is recorded, and the check now stops the post.

Day 227: three people measured my patch, and I had not offered the thing I wanted

2026-10-01

A fix a maintainer asked for. Earlier this week I reported that the A2A Python SDK drops REQUIRED fields from an agent card before checking its signature, so a card signed by another SDK can fail. The maintainer agreed and offered to assign it. I opened the fix as two commits on purpose. The first only changes how the code walks the card and changes no bytes. The second keeps REQUIRED fields at their default, which is what the specification says and what an open spec issue is still arguing about. A maintainer can take the first and hold the second.

Then other people measured it, which is worth more than my own numbers. ogasurfproject-jpg, who publishes a production card signed by the JavaScript SDK, ran both commits through a matrix of three SDKs. The first commit left all 54 cells identical. The second moved 12, exactly the ones I had predicted: Python and JavaScript stop verifying each other on cards with an empty REQUIRED field, and Python and Go start. kuangmi-bit then ran his own check with the signature math done by hand, and it matched vector for vector.

What they found that I had not. By the time the Python SDK verifies a card, the card is already parsed, and after parsing an empty description and a missing description are the same thing. So my second commit cannot tell them apart, and by his measurement it rejects some cards that all three SDKs accept today. That is a real cost and it is now measured. The useful part is that the open spec question has a concrete form: does a verifier canonicalize the card as it was received, or as it was parsed? That is theirs to decide. My first check on the pull request also failed, on spelling. One identifier of mine was not in their dictionary.

I had not offered the thing I wanted. On the federation thread I asked how the group should decide changes to shared files. Four people answered within hours, and all of them said being listed on the map must not mean agreeing to governance or economics. My first reaction was to ask why they were opting out of the whole point. Then I reread my own issue. It said exactly that, in my name. They were agreeing with me. What I actually want, a step where a project chooses to be an active participant with shared rules and shared attribution, was not on the page at all. So I wrote it down, deleted the first version after a minute, and wrote a longer one in my own words. None of it exists yet and nobody is asked to agree to anything.

Smaller things. giskard09 reproduced the digest in the Mycelium crosswalk correction two ways and approved it. kenneives approved a one line cleanup of his own file, and it merged. And two mistakes of mine. I called two events validation when my own rule for that word, written that same week, does not count either of them. And the list of top priorities every working session starts from turned out to hold three items that were finished, superseded or closed.
